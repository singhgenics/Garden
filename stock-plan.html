Stock plan

Every ingredient and consumable the menu needs, where it lives when it is sealed (bulk in the store room, cold stock in the kitchen and bar), where it goes when it is in use, how it rotates, and a count sheet for opening and closing. Built from the food menu, the drinks menu, and the kitchen, store room and bar in the equipment plan. The kit is a lean start-up kit, so cold storage is one dual-zone upright and two small chillers; the store room carries everything that does not need cold.

1 · How it works

Two homes for every item

Each item has a home, where sealed stock sits (a store-room rack, a chiller shelf, the locked spirits cage), and a working spot, where the open pack or the day's issue sits in the kitchen or at the bar. You count the home and the working spot together as one number.

Three kinds of count

  • Daily, opening and closing: chilled and frozen food, eggs, dairy, kegs, and each bar bottle (open bottles in tenths).
  • Weekly, Monday opening: the full store room, rack by rack, top to bottom; then the kitchen and bar units.
  • Monthly: everything, with the sealed-stock value for the books.

Marks on the list

  • M Named or clearly implied by a dish on the menu.
  • A A standard recipe component the menu does not name. Check these against your recipe cards.
  • P Made in house, on the prep list in section 6.
Order rule. Order = par minus the closing count. Fresh items are ordered daily for the next morning. Dry goods are ordered weekly, when the closing count falls to half of par.

2 · Store room

Laid out for rotation. Every shelf is reached from the aisle, so nothing hides behind anything. Fast-moving stock is nearest the door: oils and sauces, rice and noodles, drinks, vegetable crates. Slow stock is further in: spices and tins, bar dry goods, paper. Spirits and chemicals are locked at the far end. Heavy tins, sacks and cases go on the bottom shelf, bottles and jars above. New stock goes behind or beneath the old (section 7).
It has room to spare. The six racks give roughly 6,000 L of shelf space. The dry goods, packaging and ambient vegetables on this list need roughly 400 L at the weekly restock (my estimate), so you can order dry goods fortnightly or monthly for a better price. Heavy things (oil tins, rice sacks, beer cases) are the limit on cash, not on space; only buy a month ahead what keeps a month.
The kitchen and bar keep working stock only. The units below hold what is in use for two to three days; refill them from the store at opening. They are not counted as homes on the sheet; the store racks are.
Air, pests and heat. Two windows on the east wall, one at each corner, let hot air out and a grille low in the door lets cool air in (fit fly mesh and keep the sun off the stock), so onions and potatoes do not sweat. Keep the door closed to kitchen steam, with a brush strip under it, and look for droppings and damp at every weekly sweep.
Not in the store room. Anything that needs a chiller or freezer, the LPG cylinders (outside, open air) and spare kegs for more than 48 hours (they need a cold place; a keg room or cold room is the later fix). Spare kegs are a standing weekly delivery with a swap on the day one runs out.

3 · Chillers, freezers and the bar

Shelf numbers run from the top (1) to the bottom. Ready-to-eat goes on top, raw chicken on the bottom, so nothing drips onto food that is not cooked again.

Prep surfaces. Raw meat and fish come out of K6 and are prepared only on the K8 worktop (red and blue boards). Vegetables come out of K8 and are prepared only at the K10 bench on the west wall (green board), at the veg wash sink. Dry goods are never opened on either surface.

4 · Restock rhythm

Every item grouped by how often it is ordered. Order the day before it is needed; fresh items go in the evening order, dry goods in the weekly one.

5 · Stock list and count sheet

Type the opening count, what came in, and the closing count. The sheet works out what was used and what to order. It saves on this device for each date.

7 · Stock rotation

Rotation means old stock is used before new stock, so nothing sits at the back until it spoils. It has three habits: date it, shelve it new-behind-old, and check it on a schedule.

The one rule. First in, first out (FIFO). Where an item has a use-by date, first expired, first out (FEFO) comes before anything else: the nearest date is used first, even if it arrived later.

Every delivery, in this order

  1. Check the date, the temperature (chilled 5 °C or below, frozen -18 °C or below) and the packaging. Refuse anything warm, damaged or short-dated.
  2. Label. Write the item, the date received and the use-by date on a day-colour label (see below) on every box, tub and bag. Opened packs get the date opened too.
  3. Shelve chilled and frozen goods first. Take the old stock off the shelf, wipe the shelf, put the new stock at the back, and put the old stock in front.
  4. Write the quantity into the In column of the count sheet.

Day-colour labels

Use one colour of tape or sticker for each day of the week, the same colour all over the kitchen and bar. Anyone can see at a glance which tub is oldest without reading a date. Pick the seven colours once and write them on the wall of the kitchen.

In the store room

  • Fill from the aisle, never from behind. New cartons go to the back of the shelf or beneath the old ones; the oldest is always at the front, with its day-colour label showing.
  • Keep a USE FIRST slot at the front of the bottom shelf of each rack. Anything within a week of its date moves there, and the next order is reduced to match.
  • Bring only what the working units (U1, U4, K12, the bar) need for two or three days, oldest first, at opening.
  • Keep everything 10 cm off the floor and 5 cm off the walls so air can move and the floor can be mopped. Spirits and chemicals stay locked.

The "use first" tray

  • Keep one clearly marked tray or shelf in K6 and one in K8 called USE FIRST.
  • At closing, move anything that will reach its date within two days onto it. The chef cooks from that tray before anything else, and plans a special around it if there is a lot.
  • Nobody puts new stock on this tray.

Weekly sweep (once a week, same day, with a second person)

  1. Empty one unit at a time: K6, K8, K7, then each store rack in turn (SR-W1 to SR-E3), then the bar units.
  2. Throw out anything past its date or in poor condition. Write each item on the waste log below.
  3. Wipe the shelves and the inside of the door.
  4. Put stock back with the oldest at the front.
  5. Check pests: droppings, chewed packs, damp. Tell the owner the same day.
  6. Count the waste and ask why. If one item is thrown out every week, lower its par (section 5).

8 · Opening and closing routine

Opening, before prep

  1. Open every kitchen cabinet and look for damp, pests and spills. Check the bar and the dish-wash cabinet too.
  2. Read the chiller and freezer displays and write them on the log. Chilled should be 5 °C or below, frozen -18 °C or below.
  3. Receive deliveries. Check temperature, date and packaging, and put chilled and frozen goods away first. Write the quantities into the In column.
  4. Count the opening stock: K6, K8, K7, the bar fridge and each bar bottle. Write it in the Open column.
  5. Top up the working units (U1, U4, K12, B4, the bar) from the store room in one trip, oldest stock first.
  6. Thaw tomorrow's frozen prawns, wontons and bao in K6, not on the bench.
  7. Check the fryer oil level and colour in both tanks, and the gas bank gauge.
  8. Bar: change the keg if one is empty, check the CO₂ gauge, tip the day's bagged ice into the B7 well, and chill the vodka for shots in B9.

Closing, after last order

  1. Count the closing stock into the Close column, in the same order as the sheet.
  2. Wrap, label and date everything open. Throw out anything past its use-by date and write it on the waste log.
  3. Move rice to a tray to cool, and put cooked pork, broths and sauces in the chiller within 90 minutes.
  4. Filter the fryer oil. Change it when it is dark or smells. Record each change.
  5. Print or read the Order list, and send it to the suppliers.
  6. Put anything unopened back in its unit, labels facing out, oldest at the front.
  7. Close the gas at the cylinder bank, and check the hood and fryer controls are off.
  8. Lock B8, the spirits cage and the chemical cupboard in the store room, and lock the cash drawer B17a. Check the gas is off at the cylinder bank.

Rules that keep the count honest

  • One person counts, and a second person checks the bar and the pork and prawn lines once a week.
  • First in, first out: new stock goes behind old.
  • Nothing leaves a locked unit without being written on the sheet.
  • Weigh an open bottle by eye in tenths. Count a full bottle as 1.0.
  • Happy hour (12–6 pm) drives the lager and Crystal Clear numbers. Weekends need about 1.6 times a weekday.

9 · What is not verified

  • Every quantity is my estimate from the menu, for 60 covers a day and an assumed dish mix. It is not from your recipe cards or sales. Change the covers box to scale it, then adjust each par after two weeks.
  • Items marked A come from standard recipes. Check them against your own.
  • Shelf lives and temperatures are typical values, not FSSAI limits. Check them with your food-safety consultant or your licence conditions.
  • Where kegs, ice cream and bao come from and in what pack size is a choice for your suppliers. Pack sizes here (20 L kegs, 5 L ice-cream tubs, 1 L coconut-milk packs) are assumptions.
  • The unit sizes assume the re-measured kitchen (4.10 × 4.18 m clear). The U4 open base under the pass table (1.15 × 0.45 m) now holds the working dry stock the old U2 shelves did, so it is tight: bring only two days of rice, noodles and flour at a time. Measure the bays on site.
  • Shelf lives are typical values for sealed and opened food, not FSSAI limits. The restock rhythm assumes your suppliers will deliver that often, which I haven't checked.
  • Cold space is tight by design. K8 is a 2-door unit of about 250 to 280 L gross and holds about 200 L of food; the K6 chiller side (about 300 L) takes overflow on its second shelf. If both are full, order vegetables daily or add a second under-counter chiller. The freezer side (about 300 L) holds about 130 L; add an under-counter freezer if it fills.
  • The store-room shelf space (about 6,000 L) and the weekly dry-stock need (about 400 L) are my rough estimates.
