Stock plan

Every ingredient and consumable the menu needs, where it lives when it is sealed (bulk in the store room, cold stock in the kitchen and bar), where it goes when it is in use, how it rotates, and a count sheet for opening and closing. Built from the food menu, the drinks menu, and the kitchen, store room and bar in the equipment plan. The kit is a lean start-up kit, so cold storage is one dual-zone upright and two small chillers; the store room carries everything that does not need cold.

1 · How it works

One home for every item

Each item has a home, where sealed stock sits (a store-room rack, a chiller shelf, the locked spirits cage), and a working spot, where the open pack sits in the kitchen or at the bar. Count the home and the working spot together as one number.

Two counts

  • Every night, at close: chilled and frozen food and bottled beer. This is the default view of the sheet, and the only thing you order from each day.
  • Every Monday, at opening: everything, rack by rack: dry goods, packaging, spirits and chemicals. The POS takes each pour and dish off stock from the recipes, so the weekly bottle count only has to confirm it.

Marks on the list

  • M Named or clearly implied by a dish or drink on the menu.
  • A A standard component the menu does not name. Check it against your recipe cards.
  • P Made in house, on the list in section 6.
Order rule. Order = par minus the closing count. There are only three rhythms: daily for fresh food, twice a week for vegetables, eggs and dairy, and weekly for everything else (dry goods, bottles, frozen, paper).

2 · Store room

Laid out for rotation. Every shelf is reached from the aisle, so nothing hides behind anything. Fast-moving stock is nearest the door: oils and sauces, rice and noodles, drinks, vegetable crates. Slow stock is further in: spices and tins, bar dry goods, paper. Spirits and chemicals are locked at the far end. Heavy tins, sacks and cases go on the bottom shelf, bottles and jars above. New stock goes behind or beneath the old (section 7).
It has room to spare. The six racks give roughly 6,000 L of shelf space. The dry goods, packaging and ambient vegetables on this list need roughly 400 L at the weekly restock (my estimate), so you can order dry goods fortnightly or monthly for a better price. Heavy things (oil tins, rice sacks, beer cases) are the limit on cash, not on space; only buy a month ahead what keeps a month.
The kitchen and bar keep working stock only. The units below hold what is in use for two to three days; refill them from the store at opening. They are not counted as homes on the sheet; the store racks are.
Air, pests and heat. Two windows on the east wall, one at each corner, let hot air out and a grille low in the door lets cool air in (fit fly mesh and keep the sun off the stock), so onions and potatoes do not sweat. Keep the door closed to kitchen steam, with a brush strip under it, and look for droppings and damp at every weekly sweep.
Not in the store room. Anything that needs a chiller or freezer, the LPG cylinders (outside, open air). Bottled beer beyond the cases on the pallet needs a chiller.

3 · Chillers, freezers and the bar

Shelf numbers run from the top (1) to the bottom. Ready-to-eat goes on top, raw chicken on the bottom, so nothing drips onto food that is not cooked again.

Prep surfaces. Raw meat and fish come out of K6 and are prepared only on the K8 worktop (red and blue boards). Vegetables come out of K8 and are prepared only at the K10 bench on the west wall (green board), at the veg wash sink. Prepared vegetables wait in lidded pans on the K33 rail at the back of K8. Dry goods are never opened on either surface.

4 · Restock rhythm

Every item grouped by how often it is ordered. Order the day before it is needed; fresh items go in the evening order, dry goods in the weekly one.

5 · Stock list and count sheet

Each night, type what came in and the closing count. The sheet works out what was used and what to order. The default view is the nightly list; switch to the full list on Mondays. It saves on this device for each date.

6 · House-made items

Typical shelf lives for chilled food stored at 5 °C or below. These are working values to check with your food-safety consultant. Label every tub with the item, the date made and the use-by date.

Prep stations: chopped and chilled, ready for service

Pan-Asian wok cooking and craft cocktails are both fast, so the chopping is done before service and held cold, not during it. Kitchen: the K33 chilled prep rail at the back of the K8 worktop. Bar: the B22 chilled garnish rail on the bartender work slab. Both hold covered 1/3 and 1/6 pans at 4 °C or below, every pan dated, and both are prepped fresh each day. The quantities are starting estimates for about 60 covers: adjust them after two weeks.

Where it is chopped. Vegetables are cut at the K10 bench on the green board (garlic, ginger and spring onion can go in the electric chopper K17, chilli and garlic in the hand chilli chopper K18, both on the same bench) and carried to K33 in lidded pans, so nothing is chopped on the K8 worktop (raw meat and fish stay at the front on the red and blue boards). At the bar, cut fruit on its own board and knife, never on a board that has touched anything else. Never keep chopped garlic or ginger in oil at room temperature: keep it dry, covered and cold, and use it within the shelf life below.

7 · Stock rotation

The one rule: oldest first. New stock goes behind old. If an item has a use-by date, the nearest date goes first, even if it arrived later.

Three habits

  1. Date it. At the delivery, check the temperature (chilled 5 °C or below, frozen -18 °C or below) and refuse anything warm, damaged or short-dated. Then write the item, the date in and the use-by date on every box, tub and bag, and the date opened on every open pack. House-made tubs get the date made and the use-by date from section 6.
  2. Shelve new behind old. Chilled and frozen go away first. Pull the old stock forward, wipe the shelf, put the new stock at the back. In the store room, fill from the aisle, keep everything 10 cm off the floor, and keep spirits and chemicals locked. Write the quantity in the In column.
  3. Keep a USE FIRST shelf. One marked shelf in K6 and one in K8. At closing, move anything that reaches its date within two days onto it. The chef cooks from it before anything else. Nobody puts new stock on it.

8 · Opening and closing routine

Opening, before prep

  1. Look in every cabinet for damp, pests and spills. Read the chiller and freezer displays and write them on the log: chilled 5 °C or below, frozen -18 °C or below.
  2. Receive deliveries: check, date, shelve (section 7), and write the In column.
  3. Top up the working units (U1, U4, K12, B4, the bar) from the store room in one trip, oldest first.
  4. Thaw tomorrow's frozen prawns, wontons and bao in K6, not on the bench.
  5. Check the fryer oil and the gas gauge. Bar: check B5, B24 and the ice-stock freezer B23 are cold and full, tip the day's ice into the B25 ice well.
  6. Mondays only: do the weekly sweep and full count.

Closing, after last order

  1. Count the nightly list into the Close column.
  2. Wrap, label and date everything open. Move anything within two days of its date to USE FIRST. Bin anything past it and log it.
  3. Cool rice on a tray, and put cooked pork, broths and sauces in the chiller within 90 minutes.
  4. Filter the fryer oil. Change it when it is dark or smells.
  5. Press Make order list and send it to the suppliers.
  6. Gas off at the cylinder bank, hood and fryer off. Lock the B25 drawer, the spirits cage, the chemical cupboard and the cash drawer.
  • One person counts; a second person checks the bar and the pork and prawn lines once a week.
  • Nothing leaves a locked unit without being written down.
  • Count an open bottle by eye in tenths; a full bottle is 1.0.
  • Happy hour (12–6 pm) drives the Kings and Budweiser numbers. Weekends need about 1.6 times a weekday.
